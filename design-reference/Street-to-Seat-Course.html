Start Here

You already have the instinct. People find you. That is not a skill most people have and it is not something a course can teach. What a course can do is give you the vocabulary the rest of this world uses, the practices that have been tested by people who do this for a living, and a system that runs when you are not standing in the lobby.

That last part is the point. A greeter makes one person feel welcome. A guest experience lead builds a team of thirty people who make everyone feel welcome, every week, whether or not she is there. Read every module through that filter.

The Win For This Course

Valor has a written, repeatable path from the parking lot at Excel Academy to a person's second visit, and a trained team that can run it without you.

How to work through it

  • One module a week. Read it, do the one action step at the bottom, then move on. Do not binge it.
  • Modules 1 through 4 are fundamentals. They are what any volunteer needs. Modules 5 through 8 are leadership and fine tuning.
  • Every module has a "Train Your Team" block. That is the part you lift out and teach in a huddle. You can hand modules 1, 3, 4 and 5 to a brand new volunteer as their onboarding.
  • Every claim has a source. If it is someone's opinion, this course says so. If it is a number nobody can trace, this course says that too.

Read this before you repeat any statistic

Church hospitality material is full of confident numbers. Guests decide in 7 minutes. In 10 minutes. In 5 minutes. In 7 seconds. Almost none of them trace back to an actual study, and in at least one case the same author published two different numbers in two different documents. This course tells you who said what and whether it is research or a professional opinion. Use the practices. Be careful with the numbers. Nothing kills credibility with a smart volunteer faster than a stat that falls apart when they Google it.

01

Why Welcome Is
Theology, Not
Customer Service

Fundamentals · 20 minutes · Give this to every volunteer
The Win

Your team can say why they are standing in a parking lot in the cold, and the answer is not "so the church grows."

Most greeter training starts with the handshake. Start here instead, because a volunteer who understands why will stay for years and a volunteer who only knows the mechanics will quit in six months.

The word behind the word

The New Testament term translated "hospitality" in Romans 12:13 and Hebrews 13:2 is philoxenia. It is built from two words: phileo, to love, and xenos, stranger. Literally, love of stranger. It is not the same as entertaining friends. The whole point of the word is the person you do not know yet.

Tagging this claim: the Greek roots are directly verifiable in any lexicon and the reading is standard. Treat the etymology as solid, the devotional application as interpretation.

The four texts most first impressions curricula use

The Illinois Baptist State Association built the only free, full, session by session first impressions curriculum available, and it anchors on exactly four passages. Worth knowing what they are and why they picked them.

TextWhy it is used
Matthew 9:9Jesus calls Matthew at the tax booth. Relationship offered before reputation is repaired. Greeting is a discipleship act, not a customer service act.
Romans 15:7"Welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you." The standard for the welcome is the welcome we received.
Hebrews 13:2Entertaining angels unaware. You do not know who is walking in.
John 13:35They will know you by your love for one another. A guest reads the room before they hear the sermon.

Three more that these curricula skip and Valor should not: Romans 12:13 (practice hospitality), 1 Peter 4:9 (show hospitality without grumbling, which is a volunteer culture verse), and Luke 14:12-14 (invite the people who cannot repay you, which is the anti self interest verse).

The question a guest is actually asking

Greg Curtis, who leads first steps at Eastside Christian Church and teaches assimilation nationally, says the unconscious question in any new environment is "Is there anyone here like me?" and that in a church "that starts in the parking lot." His colleague Tommy Carreras adds the follow up question: "Is anyone here worth imitating?"

Both of those get answered before the service starts. Neither of them is answered by a sermon.

Churches fail because they design for information transfer and not relationship building. Tommy Carreras, Eastside Christian Church, on the unSeminary podcast

What This Looks Like at Valor

Valor exists to interrupt drift. Someone drifting does not walk in looking for a program. They walk in looking to see if this place is real and if anyone here is like them. Our tagline is Stop Drifting, Build Resilient Faith. The front door is where a drifting person decides whether that is a slogan or a description.

We also meet in a school. Nobody walks past a steeple and wanders in. Every single person who shows up on a Sunday made a decision to drive to Excel Academy. That raises the stakes on the first ninety seconds, because they already spent something to get here.

Train Your Team On This

Ask your team, one at a time, in a huddle: "What was your first negative experience at a church?" Rich Birch uses that exact question because it flips a team out of tactics and into memory. Most volunteers have a story. Once they tell it, they stop greeting from a script.

Then land the line Greg Atkinson tells teams to say in the huddle every single week: "It is always someone's first Sunday."

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Write three sentences on why you personally took this role. Not the job description. The reason. You will use it every time you recruit.

Map the Journey
Before You Fix It

Fundamentals · 30 minutes · Do this one with a notebook
The Win

You have walked Valor's Sunday as a stranger would, in order, and written down every place a guest could get stuck.

Where the phrase comes from

"Street to the seat" traces to Nelson Searcy's book Fusion: Turning First Time Guests into Fully Engaged Members of Your Church (Regal, 2008). Chapter three is titled "Seven Minutes and Counting," and Searcy breaks the arrival into four moves a guest needs: Greeted, Directed, Treated, Seated.

That four word chain is the most useful thing in the whole first impressions world. Memorize it. Every failure on a Sunday morning is one of those four missing.

About the seven minutes

Searcy wrote: "Seven minutes is all you get to make a positive first impression." He states it as a conviction from his own church's guest surveys. He does not cite a study, and no study exists. Other well known voices give different numbers. Greg Atkinson says ten minutes. ServeHQ says five. One curriculum says seven seconds in one document and seven minutes in another. Use the concept, which is real and obvious: the decision happens early, before the preaching. Do not quote a number as research.

The bigger map: three journeys

Greg Curtis frames the whole assimilation path as three journeys, named from the guest's point of view rather than the church's org chart. This is the frame worth adopting because it tells you where your lane ends.

JourneyWhat it is
Screen to SeatThe BELONGING journey. Website, social, parking, greeters, kids check in, ushers. Curtis notes many churches report guests watch online several times before ever attending in person, which is why he changed "street" to "screen."
Seat to CircleThe GROWING journey. Into a group, a team, or one program where formation actually happens.
Circle to StreetThe GOING journey. Equipped to live and witness outside the building.

What This Looks Like at Valor

Your lane is journey one and the handoff into journey two. Concretely: everything from the moment someone Googles Valor through their arrival at Discover Valor, which is step two of our pathway. Nathan Ewing owns Next Steps, which is step three. The exchange zone between you and Nathan is Discover Valor into Next Steps.

Valor's documented pathway, in order: Meet the Pastor (informal, right after service, non communion Sundays only) into Discover Valor (30 minute class) into Next Steps (Nathan) into All In (membership, quarterly, off site). Any pastoral need that surfaces in your lane routes to Nathan or Justin. You are not carrying pastoral care.

The exercise: walk it with a new nose

Greg Atkinson's governing metaphor for this work is smell. He painted a hallway in his house, stopped noticing the paint fumes within hours, then walked back in from outside and got hit with it all over again. His instruction to church leaders: "As best you can, try to walk into the lobby or entrance of your church with a new nose."

Here is his actual route as a paid secret shopper, in order. Copy it.

  1. Days before. Website. Facebook. Instagram. Google. He weights this so heavily he once wrote thirteen report pages on online presence before ever driving to the campus.
  2. Parking lot. Where do I park. Is anyone outside. Is there a sign.
  3. Restrooms. He goes straight there. In rented venues he says you may need your own cleaning crew hitting the bathrooms before people arrive.
  4. Ushers and security. Who noticed me. Who did not.
  5. Nursery and kids space. Clean, safe, secure. Those are his three checkpoints.
  6. Auditorium. Last, not first.

He deliberately loiters in the low attention zones, because that is where the truth is. And he is honest about the limit of self auditing: a person who already belongs cannot fully see it. If you can, send someone who has never attended Valor and have them walk it cold.

Train Your Team On This

Take your team to secret shop another church. Rich Birch says two mistakes will ruin it: assuming the other church is just like yours, and assuming their failures could never happen at yours. Scout ahead, give each person two specific things to notice, then debrief over lunch. He also suggests a synagogue or a mosque, because it puts your team in the genuinely disoriented seat: shoes on or off, do I stand, do I sit, where do I go. That is what a guest feels.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Walk Valor's Sunday in Atkinson's order, alone, with a phone note open. Write one line per stop. Do not fix anything yet. Just document it.
  • Mark every stop with Greeted, Directed, Treated, or Seated. Circle the missing ones.

The Visit Starts
on a Screen

Fundamentals · 25 minutes · Give this to every volunteer
The Win

Someone who has never met us can find our address, our service time, what to wear, and what happens to their kid, in under sixty seconds and without scrolling.

The first impression happens days before the handshake. Atkinson evaluates online presence before he ever gets in the car. Curtis renamed the whole first journey "screen to seat" for this reason.

What has to be findable

Rich Birch's list of what a church website must have for a first time guest: address and service times, past messages, kids ministry information, and a clear sense of what this church actually cares about. He cites survey work suggesting roughly half of new visitors check the website first.

  • Address and service time above the fold. Not on a contact page. On the homepage, without scrolling. Ours: 11500 W 84th Ave, Arvada, CO 80005. Sundays at 10a.
  • What to expect. Interior photos, not exterior. A guest wants to see the room and the people, not the sign.
  • Kids information that answers the actual fear. Not "we love kids." Rather: how check in works, who is with my child, how I get them back.
  • Past messages. They will sample the preaching before they sample the parking lot.

Social is a preview, not a bulletin board

Birch's guidance: post what to expect content. Interior photos. Live video. Weekly highlight videos. The job of the feed is to lower the unknown, not to announce events to people who already come.

IBSA suggests a simple three post weekly rhythm that a volunteer can run: message recap, midweek story, weekend invite. That is a rhythm someone can hand off.

What This Looks Like at Valor

We are a mobile church in a school building, which means online is doing more work for us than for a church with a building on a corner. Someone driving past Excel Academy on a Tuesday learns nothing about Valor. The website and the feed are the building.

Our handle is @myvalorchurchco and the URL is valor.church, always lowercase. Kate Wojick manages Google Business Profile and social scheduling, so the online front door is a shared lane. Your job is not to run the accounts. Your job is to be the person who says "a first time guest cannot find X" and get it fixed.

Train Your Team On This

Hand a volunteer your phone and say: "You have never heard of this church. Your kid is four. Find out where to go on Sunday and what happens to your kid." Watch without helping. Time it. Whatever they struggle with is the fix list.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Run the phone test above on valor.church with two people who do not attend Valor. Write down every question they could not answer.

04

Parking Lot
to Lobby

Fundamentals · 30 minutes · Give this to every volunteer
The Win

A stranger never has to ask a single question to find the door, the coffee, the bathroom, or their kid's room.

The rules that are not negotiable

These come from Greg Atkinson's secret shopper work and Life.Church's greeter guidelines. They are behavioral, they are teachable in ten minutes, and violating them is what shows up in every negative report.

  • Hands free. No coffee cup. No phone. Atkinson: "If your team member is distracted by looking at their cell phone, it is one of the rudest and worst first impressions you can give a newcomer." Hands are for doors, handshakes, and pointing the way.
  • Do not talk to each other. Parking attendants spread out, not bunched up. Two volunteers in conversation while a guest walks past is the single most common failure.
  • Escort, do not point. Life.Church's written guideline: "Personally escort any guest who asks where something is located. Avoid pointing." Walk them.
  • Greet the car, not the person. Wave and smile at vehicles pulling in. The welcome starts before they park.
  • Four words for anyone who looks lost or looks off: "May I help you?" Atkinson teaches this as hospitality and as a security posture at the same time.
  • Be there when it ends. Teams at their posts after the service to say goodbye, not just hello. Atkinson calls this "finish strong."

Signage: design for three distances

Birch's framing is the most practical thing written on church signage. Every exterior sign has to be readable at three distances: from the moving car, from about thirty five feet out, and standing at the door. One sign rarely does all three. Count every exterior door and ask what a guest sees at each one.

Inside, wayfinding signage has to answer exactly four questions immediately: where do I drop my kid, where is coffee, where is the bathroom, where is the service. If those four are not answered in the first ten feet, you have a signage problem, not a friendliness problem.

The unglamorous list

  • Bathrooms. Birch says wipe down counters multiple times per Sunday. Atkinson says in a rented venue you may need to bring your own crew. Both are talking about us.
  • Declutter the lobby. Birch's benchmark is a good airport. Assign the decluttering to a person, not to everyone.
  • Put away the backstage stuff. Vacuum, stacked chairs, cases, cables.
  • Smell. It is an actual chapter in Atkinson's book. You cannot smell your own building.
  • Identifiable team. Shirts or lanyards that stand out. The goal is that a guest can spot a helper from across a room, not that volunteers get swag.

What This Looks Like at Valor

We load in and tear down every single Sunday out of two connex trailers. That means our signage has to be portable, our lobby is a school hallway we do not control, and our bathrooms were used by teenagers on Friday. None of that is an excuse. It is a job description.

Two specific implications. First, signage is a setup task with an owner and a checklist, not something someone remembers. Second, teardown starts while guests are still in the building. Someone stacking a road case behind a family who is deciding whether to come back is a real cost. Protect a clean fifteen minutes after service before visible teardown starts.

Address on every sign and every post: 11500 W 84th Ave, Arvada, CO 80005. Sundays at 10a.

Train Your Team On This

Run role plays. IBSA's free workbook has four drills already written: Lost and Late, New and Alone, Multilingual Barrier, The Reserved Teen. Ten minutes in a huddle, two volunteers, everyone else watches. It feels awkward once and then it is the most useful thing you do all month.

And say this out loud to the team, because Atkinson says every guest services team secretly believes the opposite: this is not the B team. Volunteers assume they are at the door because they cannot sing or run sound. Overcorrect that in public.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Stand at the main entrance for one full Sunday and count how many people have a phone or a coffee in their hand while a guest walks past. Do not correct anyone that day. Bring the number to the next huddle.

05

What to Actually
Say to a Stranger

Practice · 25 minutes · Give this to every volunteer
The Win

A nervous volunteer has three questions in their pocket and knows the one goal is a name, not a sale.

Redefine the win out loud

The best line in all of this material is Tommy Carreras describing what he tells a volunteer who thinks their job is to sell the church:

I got to sell the church. Nope. No, no, no. You got to remember their name and ask three personal questions about them. That is my goal for you. Just do that. Tommy Carreras, Eastside Christian Church

That is the whole module. Everything below is scaffolding for people who freeze.

F.O.R.D.

A small talk framework IBSA teaches in its welcome team guide. Four safe territories, in escalating order of intimacy.

FFriends and family. Who did you come with.
OOccupation. What do you do during the week.
RRecreation. What do you do when you are not working.
DDreams. What are you hoping for. This one is last for a reason.

Most volunteers get stuck after "how are you." F.O.R.D. gives them somewhere to go without being invasive.

Kill the jargon

Rich Birch defines jargon precisely: "a subtle social tool used to define the boundaries of who is a part of the group." That is why it matters. It is not that the words are wrong. It is that they sort people into insiders and outsiders in real time.

The phrases he names, which you can put on a card for your team:

  • "The Lord is working in my heart"
  • "During my quiet time"
  • "A lost sheep, straying from the fold"
  • "A time of communion and fellowship"
  • "Hedge of protection"
  • "Share my testimony"
  • "Burdens on my heart"
  • "Do you know where you are going to spend eternity?"
  • "Lord willing"

Note this is about the lobby, not the pulpit. Preaching gets to define its terms. A greeter does not have that luxury.

Give one next step, not a menu

Carreras again: "If you give somebody A, B, C and D, they will choose E, none of the above." Recommend one thing. Take yes or no. If it is no, ask what they are actually looking for and then recommend one other thing.

What This Looks Like at Valor

Our single recommended next step for a first time guest is Meet the Pastor, immediately after service, on non communion Sundays. It is informal, it is short, and Justin is right there. That is the smallest possible ask, and small is the point.

On communion Sundays Meet the Pastor does not happen. Your team has to know which Sunday it is before the doors open, because "come meet the pastor" on a week when it is not running is worse than saying nothing.

Train Your Team On This

Print three things on a wallet card for every volunteer: the F.O.R.D. letters, the jargon list, and the sentence "remember their name and ask three questions." Nothing else. A card with twenty items gets left in a car.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Write Valor's version of the wallet card. One side, nine lines maximum.

Kids Check-In Is
Your Real
Front Door

Practice · 25 minutes · Run this one with Lacy
The Win

A first time family is walked through check in by a named person, and their information reaches the follow up system the same way a connect card would.

The finding that should change your priorities

Tommy Carreras, on why churches miss most of their new people:

If you are not treating your new families like new guests, you are ignoring them. They are giving you all the info besides their social security number. Tommy Carreras, Eastside Christian Church

He states that thirty to forty percent of a church's new guest leads come in through kids check in. Treat that as his professional observation from working with churches, not as published research. Even discounted, the implication holds: the family that hands over a child's name, allergies, and a phone number never fills out a connect card, and most churches never count them.

The three checkpoints

Atkinson's whole evaluation of children's ministry reduces to three words: clean, safe, secure. He ranks children's ministry second only to first impressions in his weighted priority list, ahead of worship.

  • A dedicated first time check in person. Birch's item eight. Someone whose only job is new families, and who is free to leave the desk and walk with them. He acknowledges this person "might not be busy the entire morning." That is the correct use of a volunteer.
  • Walk them to the room. Atkinson: a volunteer walks the parent through registration, then that person or another helper walks them to the classroom explaining what will happen and how pickup works.
  • Explain the sticker system before it is needed. A parent who does not understand pickup will spend the entire service thinking about it.
  • Never skip the matching tag at pickup. Atkinson: "If anyone can walk into a classroom and pick up a kid, you are asking for trouble." No exceptions for people you recognize. The rule only works if it is visibly absolute.
  • Signage for kids check in starts in the parking lot. Not at the door. Birch and Atkinson both land here.
  • Roamers during check in and check out. Birch's item fourteen. Someone whose job is the parent doing the long slow walk away from a crying two year old.

What This Looks Like at Valor

Lacy McKay owns Valor Kids. This module is a shared build, not yours alone. The specific thing to solve together: a first time family currently enters our data through kids check in, but the guest follow up runs off the connect card. Those are two different doors and one of them is not wired to the follow up.

Ask Glenn Jacobsen how kids check in data routes today. He owns the systems layer, including connect card routing and Planning Center accuracy. Do not build a parallel spreadsheet. Fix the routing that already exists.

Train Your Team On This

Carreras's fix is one sentence and it costs nothing: do not wait for new families to visit the guest table. Bring the guest table to them. Station a welcome person at kids check in rather than only in the lobby.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Sit down with Lacy and Glenn for twenty minutes. One question: when a brand new family checks in a child, what happens to that family's information by Monday morning? Write down the actual answer, not the intended one.

The Card,
the Text, and
the 24 Hours

Fine tuning · 30 minutes · Leadership level
The Win

Every first time guest is contacted by a real named human within 24 hours, and nobody has to remember to do it.

Speed is the whole game

Rich Birch's item twenty, stated plainly: "If you wait until Tuesday to email people who came last weekend, move that follow up back to Sunday evening." He also ranks the channels: phone calls and handwritten notes beat email.

IBSA's free curriculum lays out the only complete four stage cadence available for free:

StageTiming and channel
Immediate0 to 24 hours. Text or email.
Intentional3 to 5 days. Phone call, handwritten note, or a welcome lunch.
Pre-weekendSaturday night text.
Ongoing2 to 3 weeks out. Coffee, group invite, or serve invite.

IBSA also names the three ways follow up dies: over automation, no relational handoff, and no clear next step. Every one of those is a system that technically ran and still failed.

Connect cards: fewer fields, more cards

Brady Shearer's rule, verbatim: "The more form fields you display on a connect card, the fewer cards you will have filled out." He cites Barna data that millennials will give a first name, roughly half are comfortable giving a last name, and only about thirty percent are okay giving an email address.

His design specifications, which are worth copying regardless of what platform you use:

  • Postcard shape, not a flyer. Reads as friendly rather than promotional.
  • Color code it and refer to it by color from the stage. "The blue card." Not "our connect card," which is jargon.
  • Break the ask into steps. Old flow: fill out this whole card. New flow: scan a QR code, then one question at a time. He cites Jotform data that one question per screen converts about thirty six percent better.
  • Phone number is optional. Say so on the form.
  • Point the QR code at a redirect URL, not the final page. Send it to valor.church/qr-connect which forwards to the real form. Now you can change the destination without reprinting a single card. This is the single most practical tip in the whole body of material.
  • Make a 16:9 screen version of the card so the online guest and the in person guest do the same thing.

The email that gets a reply

Shearer's automated first email has three deliberate design choices, and all three are transferable:

  • From a real person's name, not the church name. "Abigail from Valor," not "Valor Church."
  • Ask a question that requires a reply. His version asks the guest to reply with which charity they want the church's five dollar donation sent to.
  • The reply is the point. It connects the guest to a human, and it marks your sending address as a safe sender so future emails do not land in spam.

Worth naming: Shearer's system runs on Nucleus, which is his own product. The principles above are platform neutral and worth teaching. The tooling recommendation is a sales funnel. Take the first, ignore the second.

A number you will hear repeated

Atkinson writes that if a guest returns for a second visit "you have won 80% of the battle." Rich Birch says people who do not take a step in their first 100 days generally never connect, attributing it to "some churches that have done extensive studies." Neither names a study. Both are experienced practitioners and both may be right. State them as convictions from named people, not as findings.

What This Looks Like at Valor

Two follow up motions already exist and you inherit both. Nathan Ewing gets a notification for every guest logged through the post service connect card submission and sends a personal thank you text no later than Monday 10 AM: thanks, questions, how can I help. Separately there is a 24 hour first time guest follow up email.

Your first job here is not to add a third motion. It is to confirm those two actually fire every week, find out who is falling through, and make the handoff into Discover Valor explicit. Glenn owns the routing. Nathan owns the text. You own whether the person actually lands.

Train Your Team On This

Write the announcement script and hand it to whoever is on stage, by Wednesday. Shearer's model: hold the physical card while you say it, run a matching slide, name the card by color, and describe the mechanic in one sentence. Never let the person on stage improvise this.

Your One Action Step This Week

  • Take the last four Sundays of connect cards. For each guest, write down what actually happened and when. That gap analysis is your entire first ninety days of work.

08

Build a Team
That Outlasts You

Fine tuning · 35 minutes · Leadership level
The Win

You can be gone on a Sunday and a guest cannot tell.

This is the module the whole course was built for. Everything before it can be done by a warm person. This one cannot.

Numbers worth watching

Rich Birch's weekend metrics, useful as rough targets rather than laws:

  • New guest volume. His rule of thumb: yearly new guests should roughly equal a single Sunday's attendance. His worked example: 200 in attendance means about four new guests a weekend.
  • Volunteer percentage. Plateaued churches run around twenty percent of adults serving. Growing churches he observes run about thirty percent. Three hundred in attendance means roughly one hundred serving.
  • First time volunteers. The metric he weights most. "If your volunteer influx reaches zero, you have a pipeline problem that you will not necessarily feel the pain of today but certainly will in six months to a year." He adds that the trajectory matters more than the absolute number.

These are Birch's observations from consulting, not survey data. Use them to notice direction, not to grade yourself.

Staff above the minimum

Birch's item ten: build the team past what a Sunday strictly requires, so people can leave their post without a hole opening. Some churches designate people to "swim the lobby", meaning they have no station at all. Their assignment is to move and find the person standing alone.

A real onboarding path

IBSA's six step volunteer onboarding framework, which is the cleanest free version of this and maps directly onto what Valor needs written down:

  1. Interest and invitation. Someone asks them personally. Not a stage announcement.
  2. Orientation. What this team is for and what the win is.
  3. Shadowing. They follow someone for a Sunday with no responsibility.
  4. First serve. Paired, not solo.
  5. Feedback and coaching. Within a week, specific, in person.
  6. Integration and celebration. They are named in front of the team.

Most churches do steps one and four and wonder why volunteers evaporate.

Train the feeling, not just the tactics

Birch's argument is that guest services training fails because it teaches procedures to people who have forgotten what disorientation feels like. His five experiences, which are genuinely worth doing:

  • Take the team to play bingo. A subculture with rituals, lucky charms, reserved seats, and its own vocabulary. His point: all the trappings of a religious experience, and you will feel like an outsider inside of five minutes.
  • Secret shop another church. Scout first, assign two things to notice, debrief over lunch.
  • Have the team share their own first time experiences. Including the negative one.
  • Go to a theme park. Parking, arrival flow, strollers, accessibility, cleanliness. All of it maps.
  • Visit a synagogue or a mosque. Shoes on or off. Do I stand. Where do I sit. That is exactly what your guest feels.

The staff rule

Carreras, on leadership presence: "If you are on staff and you are hiding in the green room, fix that." Atkinson's version is a list of models he endorses for pastor accessibility: a staffed guest central spot, the pastor at the front to pray after service, or the pastor simply walking the lobby. Any of them beats disappearing.

What This Looks Like at Valor

Two dates shape everything in this module. Two services starting August 30 roughly doubles the volunteer requirement and creates a between services window that is the highest risk moment on a Sunday. And full teardown every week means your volunteers are competing with a load out for the same bodies.

Your Arranger is the reason this role exists. Do not become the best greeter at Valor. Become the person who builds a team that greets like you and runs without you. The line to hold: a role limited to greeting will not hold you, and a team that depends on you is not a team.

Lane boundaries, so they are written down. You own the front of the funnel through Discover Valor. Nathan owns Next Steps and formation. Glenn owns systems and data routing. Pastoral needs route to Nathan or Justin. You monitor back door dropoff. You do not carry pastoral care.

Train Your Team On This

Birch's staff meeting question, which is the right closing exercise for this whole course: "Which of these pieces is the weakest in our church right now, and what is the smallest move we could make in the next 30 days?" Ask it every quarter.

Appendix A

The Lingo Decoder

Every one of these will get used in a conference session or a podcast as if everyone already knows it. Now you do.

Assimilation
The whole process of moving someone from first visit to belonging. Clinical word, unavoidable in this field. Curtis prefers naming it as journeys from the guest's point of view.
First Impressions
The umbrella name for the ministry. Parking, greeting, ushering, information, kids check in. Often used interchangeably with Guest Services or Guest Experience.
Street to Seat
The arrival sequence, from the moment a guest turns off the road to the moment they sit down. From Nelson Searcy, Fusion, 2008. Curtis updated it to Screen to Seat because the visit now starts online.
Greeted, Directed, Treated, Seated
Searcy's four things a guest needs during arrival. The best diagnostic in the field.
The Front Door / The Back Door
Front door is how people come in. Back door is how they leave. "Closing the back door" means reducing dropoff after the first few visits.
Secret Shopper / Mystery Worshiper
An outsider paid or asked to attend anonymously and report on the experience. Greg Atkinson built a career doing this.
Connect Card / Communication Card
The card in the seat that captures guest information. Increasingly a QR code to a mobile form rather than a card people write on.
Next Step
The single thing you ask a guest to do next. Singular on purpose.
Swim the Lobby
A volunteer with no station whose whole job is to move through the room and find people standing alone.
Wayfinding
Signage and cues that get someone from where they are to where they need to be without asking.
Pipeline
Used two ways: the guest pipeline (visitor to member) and the volunteer pipeline (interested to serving to leading). Both need watching.
Top of Funnel
Borrowed from sales. The widest part, meaning total new people who came in. Carreras's point is that most churches count who filled out a card and never count who actually walked in.
Warm Lead
Also from sales. Someone who has already signaled interest. Some churches now invest follow up in warm leads rather than every first time attender.
F.O.R.D.
Friends, Occupation, Recreation, Dreams. A small talk ladder for volunteers who freeze.
Huddle
The short pre service team meeting. Where vision, assignments, and prayer happen. Atkinson treats it as non optional.
Philoxenia
Greek. Love of stranger. The word behind "hospitality" in Romans 12:13 and Hebrews 13:2.
