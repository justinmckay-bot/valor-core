# voice.md (Valor Church)

How Valor sounds in writing, on stage, on social, in email, in print.

## In one sentence

Direct. Pastoral. Peer-to-peer. Story first. Heavy white space. No jargon. No corporate language. No em dashes in public copy.

## Voice and tone

- Direct. Honest. Pragmatic.
- Pastoral, never soft.
- Peer-to-peer. Not "from the pulpit down to the pew." Side by side.
- Specific. Vulnerable. Real.
- Repetition for emphasis is welcome.
- Transitions heavy to hopeful without apology.
- Story first, thesis second. Always.

## Hard rules

### 1. No em dashes in public copy

Em dashes are banned in any copy that goes out publicly: emails, social, sermon handouts, marketing, congregant comms, partner comms, anything externally visible.

**Why:** em dashes are a tell for AI writing because humans rarely used them before AI tools popularized the style. Valor copy must read as written by a human pastor.

**How to apply:** when you would reach for an em dash, use a period, a comma, two short sentences, a colon, or a line break. Inside a staff member's private working files, em dashes are fine. The rule kicks in the moment copy is destined for an audience outside the staff.

### 2. No jargon

Banned words and phrases:

- "faith journey"
- "gospel-centered"
- "intentional community"
- "curated" (when used as a buzzword, not when describing actual curation)
- "lean into"
- "unpack" (as a content verb)
- "circle back"
- any phrase that sounds like a Christian conference brochure

### 3. No corporate language

Banned openers:

- "We are excited to announce"
- "We invite you to join us"
- "We are pleased to share"
- "It is our great honor to"

Replace with the actual thing. "Sunday at 10a, we baptize three." "New series starts this week. Joshua." Concrete, immediate, human.

### 4. Citation discipline

Cite biblical, theological, historical, and linguistic claims. Before delivering content with any such claim, silently tag each claim:

- (a) direct text from Scripture,
- (b) defensible interpretation with a named source, or
- (c) tradition not text.

Deliver (a) clean. Flag (b) and (c) explicitly so the reader knows which is which. If a claim cannot be sourced, do not make it.

## What good Valor copy does

- Opens with a story, an image, or a specific moment.
- Uses short sentences.
- Uses white space aggressively.
- Repeats key phrases for weight.
- Names the hard thing without softening.
- Ends with a clear invitation, not a vague hope.

## What good Valor copy avoids

- Throat-clearing introductions.
- Christianese.
- Corporate excitement language.
- Generic AI aesthetic.
- Long uninterrupted paragraphs.
- Apologizing for the Bible, doctrine, or the gospel.
- Em dashes in anything destined for an audience.
- Pious-sounding metaphors that distance the reader from God-dependent reality. Use language that sounds like a human pastor begging God to move, not a curated devotional.

## The transition move

Valor's signature rhythm: name the heavy thing honestly, then turn to hope without apology. Not "but God." Not a shortcut. The honest weight, then the honest hope. Done in two short paragraphs separated by white space.

## Approved language patterns

- "The furnace of the church" when describing prayer.
- "We're desperate for God" when describing posture.
- "Heavy lifting" when describing the work of a gathering.

## Direct feedback signals from Justin

When Justin reviews copy, his shorthand:

- "Too cheesy" means rewrite it.
- "Tighten this" means cut 30 percent.
- "Make it hit harder" means the opening is weak.
- Approval comes fast once direction is right. Do not over-explain the reasoning behind a draft.
